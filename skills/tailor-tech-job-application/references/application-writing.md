# Application writing guide

Read this file before drafting the recruiter email, LinkedIn connection note, or cover letter.

## Shared narrative

In named-company mode, public-facing writing should answer three questions:

1. Why does this company's work matter to the candidate?
2. What role outcome can the candidate help create?
3. Which verified experience makes that contribution credible?

Use `company-context.md`, `job-analysis.md`, and `candidate-evidence.md` as the factual inputs in named-company mode. In anonymous-company mode, use only `job-analysis.md` and `candidate-evidence.md`. Record the candidate's own stated interests and career intentions in the evidence ledger too. An earlier generated letter is a writing sample, not independent evidence of experience or motivation. Verify its claims against the source resume or the user's explicit factual corrections before reusing them. Give each artifact a different job:

- The email earns a reply.
- The connection note earns permission to continue the conversation.
- The cover letter explains the person's interest, the connection between their experience and this work, and why joining makes sense now.

## Voice and anti-patterns

Use compact sentences, concrete nouns, and ordinary professional language. Sound confident, interested, and informed.

Avoid:

- em dashes;
- `not X but Y`, `not only X but also Y`, and similar staged contrasts;
- generic openings such as `I am writing to express my interest`;
- empty enthusiasm such as `amazing company`, `incredible opportunity`, or `dream role`;
- stock AI phrases such as `I hope this message finds you well`, `at the intersection of`, `deeply resonates`, `unique blend`, `fast-paced landscape`, or `delve`;
- adjective piles, inflated superlatives, and claims to be the best candidate;
- repeating the company's mission statement or the resume's bullet points;
- unsupported familiarity with internal plans, culture, or team dynamics;
- unnecessary use of the candidate's first-person pronoun at the start of consecutive sentences.

Read the prose aloud. Vary sentence length naturally. Replace abstractions with the actual product, customer, system, or engineering problem when the sources support it.

Keep the planning language out of the finished letter. Show the connection through the work itself instead of repeatedly announcing `this experience is relevant`, `a practical connection`, or `a concrete reason to explore`. Each paragraph should add something the reader has not already learned.

## Recruiter email

Maximum length: 200 words including the subject line. Prefer 90 to 160 words.

Use this shape when it fits:

1. A short subject tied to the role or contribution.
2. A direct opening naming the role and one specific company signal.
3. A contribution thesis about the problem the candidate can help solve.
4. One compact proof point from the resume, interpreted for this role rather than copied.
5. A clear, low-pressure request to discuss the position.

Do not narrate multiple achievements. Do not ask for a referral in the first cold email unless the user explicitly requests it. If the recruiter is unknown, use `Hello,` or `Hello recruiting team,` rather than inventing a name.

## LinkedIn connection note

Respect the current platform limit. If it cannot be verified, stay within 200 characters.

Use one company-specific reason and one credible point of professional overlap. Make the acceptance decision easy. Do not include a cover-letter summary, a resume link, a request for a referral, or a list of technologies.

A useful shape is:

`Hi <Name>, <specific reason the company's work is relevant>. My background in <credible overlap> makes the <role/team> especially interesting. I'd value connecting.`

Adapt the shape so it does not sound templated.

## Cover letter

Write a professional letter that a candidate could comfortably say aloud to the hiring manager. The reader should understand what draws this person to the work, how they would help, and why the move makes sense. Keep it to one page, usually 250 to 400 words in four or five short paragraphs. This is a useful range, not a quota: do not pad a complete shorter letter.

### Choose the story before writing

In `candidate-evidence.md`, add a short `Cover letter narrative plan` answering:

1. **Attraction:** What specific work, user problem, or engineering responsibility draws the candidate to this role? Why does that matter to them?
2. **Connection:** What in their actual experience makes this interest understandable? Choose one relevant experience and the perspective it supports.
3. **Contribution:** How would that perspective help the people this team serves?
4. **Next step:** What responsibility or kind of work would they like to develop here, and how would the employer benefit?

Distinguish user-stated motivation from an editorial angle inferred from the overlap between the role and experience. If the candidate has given no personal explanation, draft a modest interest in the advertised work and mark that angle for review in the private plan. Do not manufacture a lifelong passion, product usage, an encounter, a turning point, dissatisfaction with their employer, or a fixed career ambition. A story can be a logical connection between real work and a next opportunity; it does not need an invented event.

Prioritize research that explains why the work is attractive. A list of company facts or impressive scale figures does not explain motivation. Verify the current job title and team; dated engineering articles establish context, not a current hiring roadmap or ownership assignment.

### Build a connected letter

Use this progression when it fits the candidate's story. Adapt the paragraph boundaries rather than filling a rigid template:

- **Why this work:** Name the role and give a specific reason for being drawn to it in the opening paragraph. Connect the company's work to a human outcome or engineering responsibility. Begin with interest, not a technical company briefing followed by `I can contribute` and a skill list.
- **Why the connection makes sense:** Explain what the candidate recognizes in this problem from their experience and the perspective they would bring. Name the actual users and work when supported. `Platform as a product`, `ownership mindset`, and `engineering excellence` need concrete meaning or should be removed.
- **One brief supporting experience:** Describe the relevant situation, the candidate's supported part in it, and what it shows about their approach. Explain why that matters here. Usually one short paragraph is enough; add a second example only when it establishes a different essential strength. Avoid consecutive paragraphs that catalogue past work.
- **Why now and what next:** Connect a modest next step to the role's actual responsibilities. Show how the candidate's development would help the team. Do not promise promotions, assert an internal career path, or overstate readiness for requirements the evidence does not cover.
- **Close:** Invite a conversation in one or two direct sentences. Add no new achievement, technology list, or second ending.

Let interest, interpretation of fit, and future contribution occupy most of the letter. Keep descriptions of past accomplishments to a supporting minority, normally one paragraph. This balance is this skill's editorial default for the user's requested voice, not a claim that all employers require a particular paragraph ratio.

### Select evidence instead of accumulating it

- Default to no quantified achievement or one useful result. Include a number only if the reader needs it to understand the example. A before-and-after comparison may express one result, but do not chain improvements, cost savings, team counts, incident counts, and SLA figures. Company scale figures also add to the reading burden.
- Keep the result's definition, unit, range, and ownership accurate. Do not merge different measures into a simpler invented percentage or change customers into internal teams. A qualitative outcome can be more useful than a numerical one.
- Explain what the example means for the prospective team. Removing digits from an achievement list does not make it a narrative.
- Use the few technical names needed to explain the connection. Java or Kubernetes can be useful; an inventory of libraries, orchestration tools, APIs, testing tools, and cloud vendors usually belongs in the resume.
- Demonstrate one or two strengths through the example. Avoid lists of self-descriptions, claims to be the best candidate, and promises of immediate ownership that the evidence does not support.
- Do not invent implementation decisions or personal lessons to make a project sound richer. Distinguish a supported account of what happened from a proposed approach to the new role.

For illustration, assume the evidence says only that an engineer introduced shared runtimes, automated deployment configuration, and supported production services:

Weak: `I delivered runtime sharing, automated deployment configuration, and handled production incidents. I bring platform expertise, reliability, and ownership.`

Stronger: `Your team's work on making deployments easier for product engineers appeals to me. My experience introducing shared runtimes gives me a practical connection to that work: a platform change affects both the service and the people who have to operate it. I would bring that perspective to making the deployment path easier to use and understand.`

The difference is the relationship between interest, experience, and contribution. These are synthetic illustrations; never copy their claims into a real candidate's application.

### Anonymous-company mode

Keep the same narrative standard when the employer is undisclosed. Start with interest in a specific responsibility or problem in `job-analysis.md`, connect one supported experience, and explain what the candidate would bring and develop. Use a neutral greeting. Omit company mission, product praise, culture claims, an invented customer domain, long-term growth inside an unknown company, and any implication that the candidate researched the employer. Missing company context is not a reason to fill the letter with achievements.

### Editorial acceptance review

Review the whole letter against the following questions. Save brief findings and any revisions under `Cover letter editorial review` in `candidate-evidence.md`; keep these notes out of the letter itself.

| Check | Accept when | Revise when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific interest | The opening explains a reason for choosing this work, beyond company prestige or scale. | It opens with a company briefing, generic enthusiasm, or capability inventory. |
| Plausible fit | The reader can follow the connection from a real experience to a useful contribution. | The reader must infer fit from tools and outcomes. |
| Evidence restraint | One brief example supports the story; past accomplishments take a minority of space. | It reads like resume bullets joined into paragraphs, even without numbers. |
| Human meaning | Technical details explain what gets easier, more dependable, or more useful for people. | Jargon substitutes for an explanation. |
| Authenticity | Experience is traceable; interest is user-stated or a modest proposed angle; no invented scenes or promises. | The letter borrows unverified claims from an earlier draft or fabricates motivation. |
| Coherence | Each paragraph advances the same reason for applying, with a clear next step and one close. | It repeats the opening, introduces unrelated projects, or bolts on a growth paragraph and extra ending. |

Apply two additional tests: after temporarily removing the achievement sentences, can the reader still explain why this person wants the role? If the company and team names were swapped, would the main argument still fit unchanged? Restore the evidence, then revise if the first answer is no or the second is yes. In anonymous mode, test swapping the role's responsibilities instead of the employer.

The validator can flag dense numbers and lists. It cannot judge sincerity, logical flow, jargon, or factual support. Passing automated checks never substitutes for this editorial review. Do not evade a warning by spelling numbers out; revise the story, or record why a necessary comparison earns its space.

## Quality test

After drafting, remove generic filler. In named-company mode, every company-specific sentence must be traceable to `company-context.md`. In either mode, every candidate claim must be traceable to `candidate-evidence.md`. An anonymous-company cover letter may use role-specific language from `job-analysis.md` without pretending to know the employer.

## Research basis and editorial choices

Reviewed 2026-09-16. The following primary career-advising sources informed the approach:

- [MIT CAPD: Career toolkit, Writing a cover letter](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/career-toolkit-writing-a-cover-letter/): reflect on one or two reasons for interest, explain what experience taught the candidate, and connect that experience to the role.
- [MIT CAPD: How to write an effective cover letter](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/how-to-write-an-effective-cover-letter/): use brief stories to establish preparedness, tailor to the employer, and keep the letter within one page.
- [Harvard Mignone Center: Create Impactful Resumes and Cover Letters](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/hes-create-impactful-resumes-and-cover-letters/): explain specific interest and fit early, then connect the candidate's story through one or two relevant examples.
- [Harvard Mignone Center: GSAS Master's Resume & Cover Letter](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/gsas-masters-resume-cover-letter/): explain both why the organization attracts the candidate and why hiring them makes sense.

These sources support motivation, selective evidence, and explicit connections. The preference for one compact example, few numbers, and little technical jargon implements this user's requested voice; it is not a universal hiring rule or evidence of improved response rates. Use the principles, not canned openings from source samples. Browse again for current platform limits or changed hiring requirements.
